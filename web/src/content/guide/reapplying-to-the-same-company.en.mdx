Short answer: **yes, you can apply again, and in most cases it does no harm.** The long answer depends on which kind of "again" you mean, because three quite different situations get called the same thing.

## Three situations, three answers

**Applying to the same posting twice.** Almost never useful. Your application is already in the system; a second one is either merged as a duplicate by the applicant tracking system or lands in the same queue again. The one exception: if something on your CV genuinely changed (a project finished, a certification earned) and the posting is still open — then you are not reapplying, you are submitting an **updated** application.

**Applying to a different role at the same company.** Entirely normal and expected. Not being right for one role does not mean the company does not want you; more often a company with a senior and a junior posting open at once simply has two different needs. There is no waiting period here at all — if the posting is open, apply.

**Applying to a role you were rejected for, when it reopens.** This is the real question, and this is where a waiting period starts to mean something.

## How long to wait

The "wait six months" advice you will find has no measurement behind it. What works better is to tie the wait **to a change rather than to a duration**:

Ask yourself what has changed on your CV since last time that the person reading it would notice. If the answer is "nothing", the length of the wait makes no difference — the outcome will be the same. If the answer is something concrete (a new job, a finished project, actually using a technology now rather than listing it), there is no reason to wait at all.

In practice:

- **If the rejection was fast or automated** — you probably hit a filter criterion and no human saw it. Reapplying with the same CV means hitting the same filter again. What needs to change is not the timing but the application.
- **If it came after an interview** — a person saw you and may remember. Here duration genuinely matters: at least a few months, and ideally to a point where you can say "I closed the gap you named last time".
- **If they said they would keep you on file** — do not treat that as a promise ([what the stock phrases in a rejection actually mean is its own subject](/guide/what-a-rejection-email-means)), but nothing stops you applying when a new posting appears.

## "Will it count against me?"

That is the common fear, and it is largely unfounded. Applicant tracking systems do keep your previous applications — but what they keep is a list of them, not a "this one applies too much" label. A recruiter seeing it does not usually draw a negative conclusion.

The one pattern that genuinely hurts: **a burst of applications to unrelated roles in a short window.** Applying to the same company's backend, sales and marketing postings in the same week leaves the question of what you are actually suited for unanswered — and that weakens every one of them.

## To know any of this, you need to be able to see your history

The practical problem with this article is that you cannot answer any of the questions above if you do not remember **when and for what** you applied to that company.

And the platforms will not tell you. On kariyer.net [older applications drop off the list](/guide/kariyer-net-application-history), on LinkedIn only Easy Apply shows, and anything sent through a company's own site is nowhere at all. Apply to one company through two platforms and neither record knows about the other.

The simplest fix is being able to group applications by company: in e-kariyerim every application to the same company sits under one row, so when you last applied and how it ended is visible at a glance. Tracking by hand, sorting the [template](/guide/job-application-tracker-spreadsheet) by its Company column does the same job.
