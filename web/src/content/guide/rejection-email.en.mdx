"We have carefully reviewed your application and have decided to move forward with other candidates."

That sentence is the standard closing line of a job application, and it says almost nothing. But knowing what it does *not* say turns out to be more useful than what it does.

## What the sentence actually means

**"We have carefully reviewed"** is often untrue. The same template goes out both to the application a person read and rejected, and to the one an automated filter dropped before any human saw it. You cannot tell which from the email.

**"We decided to move forward with other candidates"** is an outcome, not a reason. The phrasing is deliberately neutral: it makes no claim, so there is nothing in it to argue with.

**"We will keep your CV on file"** is usually not a real promise. Some companies genuinely revisit candidates; for most this is the closing courtesy of the email. Do not wait on it.

## Rejections that give a reason are the minority

A rejection email with an actual reason in it is rare, and companies avoid it deliberately: writing one takes time, and a badly worded reason carries legal risk. So a template goes out instead.

When a reason does appear, it is usually one of three:

- **Language.** "The language level required for the role" — the most common concrete reason in cross-border applications.
- **Location or work authorisation.** "Your current location", or a visa requirement. Neither is something you can fix, but both should change what you target.
- **Years of experience.** "The level of experience we are looking for." The most common and the least informative.

Worth noting: **salary expectations and technical shortcomings are almost never stated in writing.** When those are the reason, they do not appear in the email.

## The timing tells you more than the text

- **Within hours, or the same day:** automated screening. Your CV did not reach a person. Usually one filter criterion — years of experience, location, a keyword.
- **Within one or two weeks:** normal screening. It was probably read, and others were preferred.
- **All at once after the posting closed:** the pool was closed. You were not singled out; the process ended.
- **After an interview:** this is the one point where you can actually get information.

## If it came after an interview: ask

Replying to a post-interview rejection with one short question is both reasonable and often productive. Do not ask "why didn't I get it?" — that puts the reader on the defensive. Ask instead: **what would make me a stronger candidate next time?**

The second question is risk-free to answer, and you have a real chance of getting something back. It is the one concrete thing a rejection email can be turned into.

If no answer comes, leave it there.

## What a rejection is actually good for

A single rejection means nothing statistically. The **distribution** is what carries information:

- If most applications get **no reply at all**, the problem is usually that the CV is not clearing the first screen.
- If **fast rejections** dominate, you are being filtered out — read the hard requirements in the posting more carefully.
- If **post-interview rejections** dominate, the CV is working and the problem is further down the process.

Telling those three apart means counting rejections — and almost nobody counts rejections, because they all get deleted.
