Past the tenth application they all blur together. Which company, applied when, which one replied, which one has been silent for three weeks — this is not work memory can do.

There is a free spreadsheet below. But first, one thing worth knowing: most "job application tracker templates" you find online are built **for recruiters**. They are designed to track candidates arriving at one job posting, not the dozens of applications you sent out. Those two run in opposite directions.

## What is in it

Two tabs. **Applications** is the one you fill in, nine columns:

- **Company**, **Job Title** — what the application is
- **Applied At** — where the waiting clock starts
- **Status** — picked from a dropdown
- **Location** — remote/hybrid goes here too
- **Job URL** — so you still know what you applied to after the posting comes down
- **Last Activity** — when something last happened: an email, an interview, anything
- **Waiting (days)** — calculated for you, nothing to type
- **Notes** — interview notes, salary range, who you spoke to

You fill in nothing on the **Summary** tab; it is all derived from the first one: total applications, still waiting, got a reply, rejected, reply rate, and the average wait of the applications still open.

Two design choices are worth explaining:

**Waiting is counted from last activity, not from the application date.** Put a date in "Last Activity" and the clock restarts there; leave it blank and it counts from when you applied. Because what you actually want to know is not "when did I apply" but **"when did something last happen"**. An application where you interviewed three weeks ago and then heard nothing is not the same thing as one you sent three weeks ago and never heard about at all.

**A rejection counts as a reply.** The "reply rate" on the Summary tab includes rejections. That sounds backwards until you notice what it measures: not "did I get hired" but **"did a human being look at my application"**. A 5% reply rate tells you something is wrong with your CV or with the roles you are aiming at — far earlier and far more clearly than an offer count ever will.

## How to use it

Add the row **as you apply**. Reconstructing later does not work: even on LinkedIn [getting the history out is its own errand](/guide/export-linkedin-application-history), and anything you sent through a company's own site was never recorded anywhere.

Look at the Summary tab once a week and ask two questions:

1. Are there open applications past 30 days of waiting? They are not open any more. Close them. ([When to write one off is its own question.](/guide/how-long-to-wait-after-applying))
2. Is the reply rate falling? If it is, the problem is not the number of applications — it is the CV or the targeting.

## If you already have a LinkedIn history

You can paste `Job Applications.csv` from your LinkedIn data archive straight into this sheet — the columns are not identical, but company, title and date carry over directly. How to get that file is [in this article](/guide/export-linkedin-application-history).

## Where a spreadsheet stops working — honestly

A spreadsheet, even a good one, cannot do three things:

**It does not update itself.** Every rejection email, every interview invitation, you open and type in yourself. In a busy week this is the first thing to slip, and within days the sheet no longer matches reality.

**Nothing reminds you.** "This one has been silent for 21 days" is in the sheet, but nobody says it out loud unless you open the sheet.

**You learn nothing at the company level.** Three applications to the same company are three unrelated rows; seeing whether that company replies to anyone at all means grouping by hand.

Up to thirty or forty applications none of this really matters. Beyond that the spreadsheet becomes a job of its own, separate from the job hunt — and that is the real cost.
