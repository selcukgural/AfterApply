> These reviews are not a verdict on a company. They are employees' own experiences.

## Every review is one person's experience

The same company, the same period, different teams — completely different things can happen. Someone who got on well with a manager and someone who didn't may be describing the same company, and both are right. What held for one person may not hold for everyone.

## Don't decide on a single review

One review is one data point. What five reviews say means far more than what one says. Put the positive and the negative side by side: reading only the bad ones, or only the good ones, leaves the picture incomplete; together they give you the real one.

## Check the date

A review about a manager from three years ago may not describe today's team. Recent reviews show the present better; older ones show where the company has come from.

## Watch for what repeats

If five different people picked the "overtime" statement, that's not one person's bad luck. The "most often liked" and "most often could be better" lists at the top of a company page count exactly this: things that recur across independent reviews say more than any single review — on the positive side too.

## Read an area average with its count

Next to every area is how many people rated it; until enough votes are in you see "—" instead of an average. Someone who left an area blank did not find it "average" — they could not judge it — which is why a "remote work" average is shown at ten votes, not at two.

## Compare with your own expectations

What is a minus for one person — lots of travel, a fast pace, a small team — may be a plus for you. Read with your own priorities, not the writer's: does the thing that matters to you come up in these reviews, and how?
