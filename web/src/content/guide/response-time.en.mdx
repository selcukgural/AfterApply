The internet always gives the same answer: "two to four weeks on average". The trouble is that there is no measurement behind that number. Nobody counts this — not the job boards, not the companies, not the articles handing you the advice. It is an estimate passed from hand to hand.

What helps more is knowing what determines the wait, because once you see that you can work out what your own application should take.

## The four things that set the pace

**How long the posting has been open.** If it went up yesterday, the company is still filling the pool and will not screen anyone until it closes. Apply in a posting's last days and the reply comes far sooner. That alone accounts for a week of difference.

**Who makes the decision.** At a small company the person reading your CV is usually the person hiring; that runs in days. In a corporate structure there is HR, a hiring manager, budget approval, sometimes a second team — each layer adds a week. Same role, same city, four times the wait.

**Whether a human saw it at all.** Applicant tracking systems filter on set criteria. If you were filtered out, "consideration" never started: how long you wait has nothing to do with a decision and everything to do with when the company sends its batch of rejections. Sometimes it never does.

**The calendar.** Summer holidays, the end of the year and public holiday weeks genuinely stop processes. Applying in August and hearing back in September is not a delay; it is normal.

## A working rule

There is no exact number, but these thresholds hold up:

- **Days 1-7:** expect nothing. The only thing that arrives in this window is an automated acknowledgement.
- **Days 7-14:** the first real screen usually happens here. Most positive replies land in this band.
- **Days 14-30:** still possible, but the odds drop fast. This is exactly where a follow-up belongs.
- **Day 30+:** in practice it is over. Occasionally something arrives much later; you do not plan around it.

**After an interview it works differently.** Once you have interviewed, the company owes you an answer and usually names a date. If that date has passed, asking the day after is entirely reasonable — waiting here is not politeness, it is just uncertainty.

## The follow-up: when and how

Once, at the end of the second week. Keep it short, three sentences at most, and **ask a question** — an email that opens with "just wanted to check in" gives the reader nothing to reply to.

The shape that works: one sentence naming the role and when you applied, one saying you are still interested, and a question about **what stage the process is at**. A concrete question has a chance of being answered.

Do not send a second follow-up. If no reply came, that is the information.

## When to close it

Closing an application is not forgetting it; it is stopping it from taking up room at the top of your list. Past 30 days, never reached an interview stage, no answer to the follow-up — close it.

The real benefit is not emotional but measurable: **counting the applications that went unanswered is what gives you a reply rate.** If a hundred applications produce five replies, the problem is not patience — it is the CV or the roles you are targeting. You only notice that by counting the ones you closed.

## You do not have to guess at this

Everything above is generally true. What is true *for you* is a different thing: **how long your own applications actually take.** After twenty or thirty of them your own distribution appears — which sectors take longest, which company sizes reply fastest, what your own average is.

All that requires is keeping the application date and the last activity date somewhere. [Do it by hand with the template](/guide/job-application-tracker-spreadsheet), or let a tool like e-kariyerim count the waiting for you and flag anything past 30 days.

There is a more interesting question in the long run: how long do *other* people applying to the same company wait? That information exists nowhere today — making it measurable is exactly what we are working on.
