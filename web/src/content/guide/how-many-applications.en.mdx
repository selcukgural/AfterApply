Search for this and the numbers you find contradict each other. Some sources say twenty or thirty, others well over a hundred, and English-language round-ups quote figures running up to two hundred. For the Turkish market there is **no measured number at all**.

That is not because the numbers are wrong. It is because the question is incomplete.

## Why there cannot be one number

"How many applications" tries to compress three separate variables into a single figure:

**Which role.** A junior posting that draws hundreds of applicants and a niche role a company struggles to fill do not belong in the same arithmetic. Same person, same CV, tenfold difference.

**Where you apply.** An application somewhere easy to apply is cheap — for you and for the reader. Putting fifty one-click applications and five carefully written ones in the same bucket is the main thing that makes the count meaningless.

**The state of the market.** The gap between a sector in the middle of a layoff wave and one that is growing is larger than any difference between candidates.

So a number from outside tells you nothing about how you are doing. The only thing that does is **your own rate**.

## The number worth watching: reply rate

Application count measures effort. Reply rate measures whether it is **working**:

> reply rate = applications that got any human response ÷ total applications

"Response" here **includes rejections**. What you are measuring is not "did I get hired" but **"did my application reach a person"**. That distinction matters, because it separates two completely different problems:

- **Rate low (under 10%), rejections mostly fast or absent** → the problem is at the first screen: CV format, the posting's hard requirements, or the level of the roles you are aiming at. More applications will not fix this; they will just reproduce the same result.
- **Rate reasonable but nothing progresses** → the CV is working and the problem is at the interview stage. That is an entirely different piece of work.

Increasing your application count before telling those two apart is the most expensive and most exhausting strategy available.

## So what is a "normal" rate?

The honest answer: **I do not know of a reliable published figure for the Turkish market.** Putting an invented percentage in this article would have been easy, and useless.

What is known: the rate varies enormously by sector, level and how you apply, and **the direction of your own rate over time** is more informative than its absolute value. If it rises after you change your CV, the change worked. That is a sharper signal than any industry average.

## How to measure it

Three columns are enough: application date, status, last activity date. Look at it once a month.

The one requirement is **counting the ones that never answered.** This is where most people's arithmetic breaks: rejections get deleted, silences get forgotten, and only the ones that turned into interviews stay in memory. A rate computed from that memory comes out several times higher than the real one and is good for nothing.

The Summary tab of the [template](/guide/job-application-tracker-spreadsheet) does this calculation for you. In e-kariyerim the same figure sits on the dashboard, and applications that went unanswered [past a certain point](/guide/how-long-to-wait-after-applying) get flagged separately — because an uncounted silence makes the rate look better than it is.

## The one useful piece of advice about the count itself

Increasing the number of applications only works when the rate is already reasonable. Raising the count while the rate is low is just running a broken process faster.

So the order is: measure first, fix the rate second, raise the count last.
